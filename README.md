# Clinical Operations & Patient Capacity Optimization

> ### ⚠️ SIMULATED HEALTHCARE OPERATIONS
> This project runs on a **simulated** health-system operations environment built for analytical
> demonstration. Capacity, wait times, no-shows and patient-flow metrics are **modeled**. No real
> patients, providers, clinics or health systems are represented. The simulation was designed
> deliberately so the full decision chain — from driver analysis through validated forecasting to
> constrained optimisation — could be demonstrated end to end on data that carries every field the
> method requires. Provider schedules, referral demand, realised wait times and cancellations are
> not published together for any real health system.

**Domain:** Healthcare Operations · **Scale:** 26 specialty × clinic units · 8 specialties · 4 sites · 78 weeks

---

## Business Problem

A health system misses its **14-day access standard** in a large share of its clinics. Leadership's
default response is to ask how many more providers it needs to hire.

That is the expensive answer, and possibly the wrong one. The question this project answers is:

> **Where should appointment capacity actually sit to reduce patient wait times — and how much of
> the improvement is available without adding a single slot?**

## Executive Summary

Wait time was modeled against utilisation across 26 specialty × clinic units. A linear utilisation
term explains R² 0.489 of the variation; replacing it with the queueing transform ρ/(1−ρ) — the
same single variable, transformed — takes that to 0.853. Specialty and site fixed effects alone explain
only 23.6%. In this simulated system access is a **capacity** problem, not a site-culture problem.

A no-show model converts booked slots into *effective* capacity: roughly **7% of the modeled
schedule evaporates** before a patient is seen. The resulting capacity→wait curve was fitted on the
first 65 weeks and validated on a held-out 13 (R² 0.929, MAE 2.34 days), so the simulator rests on a learned
relationship rather than an assumed one.

The optimiser then allocates capacity to minimise total patient-wait-days. The headline result is
that **redistributing 5.5% of existing appointment slots cuts total wait-days 20.5%** — modeled as
equivalent to a 4.07% capacity increase, achieved without adding any slots.

It also finds the limit: **2 units cannot reach the 14-day standard at any modeled capacity level.**

## Methodology

1. **Simulate operations** — referral demand with seasonality and trend, provider schedules, no-show
   and cancellation behaviour, unit-level scheduling drag and backlog carry-over.
2. **Wait-driver analysis** — OLS on 2,028 unit-weeks with specialty and clinic fixed effects,
   comparing a linear utilisation term against the queueing transform ρ/(1−ρ).
3. **No-show model** — class-weighted logistic regression, threshold tuned on Youden's J, output as
   Low/Medium/High risk tiers. Used to convert booked slots into effective capacity.
4. **Capacity→wait curve** — re-fitted from the data with unit fixed effects, trained on weeks 1–65,
   scored on the held-out final 13.
5. **Allocation optimiser** — greedy marginal allocation. The objective is separable and convex in
   capacity, so allocating each increment to the unit with the largest marginal saving is optimal
   under the modeled assumptions.

## Key Findings

### Functional form beat adding variables

| Model | R² | Adj R² | AIC |
|---|---|---|---|
| M1 utilisation only | 0.4894 | 0.4891 | 16,516 |
| M2 + specialty & clinic FE | 0.5852 | 0.5828 | 16,131 |
| M3 queueing term + FE | 0.8532 | 0.8523 | 14,106 |
| M4 + no-show & cancel rates | 0.8533 | 0.8523 | 14,108 |

Adding no-show and cancellation rates changes R² not at all and makes AIC worse.

### No-show risk tiers

| Tier | Appointments | No-shows | Rate | vs base |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Low | 7,633 | 742 | 9.7% | 0.65× |
| Medium | 3,180 | 548 | 17.2% | 1.14× |
| High | 1,908 | 626 | 32.8% | 2.18× |

ROC-AUC **0.684**, recall 50.2% at a 15.1% base rate.

### Redistribution beats expansion

| | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Mean wait | 15.9 days | **12.6 days** |
| Units over 14-day standard | 11 | **4** |
| Slots moved | — | 5.5% of system |
| Modeled equivalent | — | a 4.07% capacity increase |

### Targeted allocation beats uniform expansion

| Capacity added | Mean wait (optimised) | Mean wait (pro-rata) | Units over standard | Optimiser advantage (wait-days/wk) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| +0% | 15.9 d | 15.9 d | 11 | 0 |
| +5% | 11.9 d | 12.8 d | 5 | 2,614 |
| +10% | 10.8 d | 11.5 d | 4 | 1,831 |
| +15% | 10.3 d | 10.7 d | 4 | 1,289 |
| +20% | 9.9 d | 10.2 d | 3 | 928 |

### Some units cannot be fixed with capacity

2 units — Dermatology @ CLN-03, Primary care @ CLN-01 — carry a structural floor of
14.4 days and 14.7 days even at zero congestion, from scheduling template drag and backlog
carry-over. **678 patients/week** sit behind those floors. Every other unit clears the standard
with **4.5%** more capacity — modeled as about **3.2 providers**.

## Decision / Recommendation

1. **Rebalance before hiring.** Move 5.5% of existing slots toward the congested units. In this
   model that alone delivers 20.5% of the wait-day reduction at no headcount cost.
2. **If capacity is added, target it.** A uniform uplift wastes a large share of the benefit — at +5%
   the optimised allocation saves 2,614 more wait-days per week for identical spend.
3. **Send Dermatology @ CLN-03, Primary care @ CLN-01 to template redesign, not to recruitment.** No modeled
   capacity level brings them inside the standard.
4. **Treat no-shows as a capacity lever.** ~7% of the schedule is lost before a patient arrives;
   recovering part of that is cheaper than buying slots.

## Validation

- **Out-of-sample validation.** Capacity→wait curve trained on weeks 1–65, scored on the held-out
  final 13: **R² 0.9293, MAE 2.34 days** against a 13.8-day mean.
- **Model comparison.** Four nested specifications compared on R², adjusted R² and AIC.
- **Jensen's-inequality control.** Baseline utilisation uses the mean of weekly ρ, not
  mean(demand)/mean(capacity). ρ is convex in capacity, so the ratio of means understates congestion.
- **Optimality argument.** Greedy allocation is optimal here because the objective is separable and
  convex — stated as a property of the model, not assumed.

## Limitations

- **The environment is simulated.** Results demonstrate a method; they are not evidence about any
  real health system.
- **Wait is generated from a queueing relationship**, then re-fitted from the data. The recovered
  fit is therefore stronger than it would be on real operational data, where referral routing,
  urgency triage and physician-level variation add noise this simulation does not fully reproduce.
- **The no-show model is deliberately modest** (ROC-AUC 0.684). It is an input to the capacity
  calculation, not a standalone product.
- **Capacity is treated as continuously divisible.** Real schedules move in provider-sessions, not
  fractional slots, so the redistribution figure is an upper bound on what a real template change
  could capture.
- **No patient-level equity analysis.** Reducing mean wait-days can in principle worsen the tail for
  specific groups; this model optimises the aggregate only.
